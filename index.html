    What am I looking at?

    You are looking at a receipt: the record of one decision. Somewhere, an AI agent asked to do one specific thing — the request described above — and a piece of safety software called the seal gate decided, at that moment, whether to allow it or refuse it. The receipt is the gate's own signed note of that single decision, and this page has just re-done the gate's arithmetic on your device to see whether the note holds up. (One special kind, the control receipt, records the opposite: a run with the gate switched off, kept to show the difference.)

    What a passing check shows — and what it does not

    A passing check shows that the receipt has not changed since it was signed and that it is internally true: feed the very same request to the very same decision software and you get the very same decision, byte for byte, right here on your device.

    A passing check does not show that the decision it describes actually happened. This page cannot see the system that produced the receipt: it cannot tell whether the request was really routed through the gate, whether the gate was in the path at the time, or what happened after the decision. A receipt can be perfectly intact and still describe a decision that never guarded anything real.

    There is a further limit, and it is there by design. This page verifies the decision over the inputs the receipt commits to — the kernel binary's hash, the fingerprint of the request, the re-derived verdict, and the exact bytes the kernel emitted. It does not check that approvals named in a receipt point at anything an operator's policy actually authorized: approval targets travel as opaque fingerprints ("commitments"), and this checker accepts receipts built around any target of the builder's own choosing — "fire-your-own-target". A valid receipt proves what was decided, never that the thing approved deserved approval.

      What this proves, and what it does not
      
      
      
        
      
          
      ✓ What this proves
      
          
      For the exact call you supplied, this is the deterministic Allow/Block decision produced by the
            seal mediation kernel whose binary sha256 is shown above, executed in your browser.
            That kernel's decision logic is the subject of public Lean 4 proofs that assert the stated
            mediation properties, modulo their stated assumptions and the axioms
            {propext, Classical.choice, Quot.sound}. Same input → byte-identical receipt, every
            reload. Nothing leaves your browser.
      
        
      
        
      
          
      ✗ What this does NOT prove
      
          
      It does not certify your whole MCP server, your transport, your tool implementations, or that your
            deployment actually routes calls through this gate. The sha256 verifies which binary
            ran. It does not prove the axioms or the Lean proofs themselves. No third party
            certifies anything here. In particular ARIA certifies nothing, no ARIA endorsement,
            outcome, affiliation, or status is claimed or implied. This is not an endorsement and not a
            trustworthiness badge for your server.
